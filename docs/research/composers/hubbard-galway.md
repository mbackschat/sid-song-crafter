# Rob Hubbard and Martin Galway: the driver as a compositional instrument

Research accessed **2026-10-12**. This study separates composer testimony, published analysis, inspected source code, archive metadata, and proposed experiments. It reads historical and current sources; it does not claim that these reference pieces were newly listened to, rendered, or traced here. The five archive files below were downloaded only to verify their headers. The proposed listening and trace exercises remain explicit next experiments.

The practical recommendation is to adopt an established SID composition editor for sound design and retain this repository's import, export, and independent verification tools. Hubbard and Galway demonstrate why faithful note timing is only the foundation: a SID instrument is often a program that changes while a note sounds, and the arrangement exploits that program. An upstream piano score should not constrain a downstream SID-native composition. The present [three-voice exporter](../../three-voice-player.md) is a verified piano-arrangement subset, not an equivalent of their composition systems.

## Hubbard: composing with a changing synthesis engine

In his [C64.COM interview](https://www.c64.com/interviews/hubbard.html), Hubbard describes three routes into a composition: keyboard experimentation, paper notation, and direct byte editing through a machine-code monitor. He also describes deliberately filling a melodic rest with another musical function on the same channel. His driver changed to suit the work, sometimes favoring memory and sometimes execution speed. This is first-person evidence for an iterative music-and-code workflow; it does not establish that Hubbard personally used a later graphical tracker bearing his name.

Matthew Vosburgh's contemporary [Electronics & Music Maker interview, April 1986](https://www.muzines.co.uk/articles/techtalk-living-off-video/9284), describes an interpreter between music data and SID registers. Hubbard explains software-generated vibrato that consumes no second oscillator, delayed vibrato combined with pitch bending, and a transient beginning in noise before changing waveform. The software controller supplies time-dependent synthesis rather than choosing one static patch at each note. For this project, the useful inference is that a better lead needs control over the beginning, body, and release of the sound, with pitch animation timed independently of the score's next note.

Anthony McSweeney's [1993 reconstruction of the Monty on the Run routine](https://www.neperos.com/article/rxmd9gd536b13923) is **reverse engineering**, not Hubbard's original labeled source. McSweeney says he removed sound-effects code for clarity. The reconstruction separates note fetching from ongoing sound processing; eight-byte instruments include pulse parameters, waveform, ADSR, vibrato depth, and effect flags. Inspection confirms an octave alternation driven by frame parity, a descending effect that decreases the saved frequency's high byte every second frame, and percussion that changes from an initial noise phase to the instrument waveform. These observations apply to this reconstructed routine, not every later Hubbard engine. Its compact flags expose a real limitation: this particular arpeggio facility is much narrower than a general programmable chord table.

James Newman's peer-reviewed [“Driving the SID chip” (2017), especially pp. 37–43](https://www.gamejournal.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/GAME_06_HearTheMusic_Journal_Newman.pdf), interprets the driver as part of the instrument. His analysis of **Commando** identifies melody, chord backing, and bass as prevailing channel duties, with percussion inserted across those channels. His analysis also connects Hubbard's descending pitch effects and octave alternation to **Monty on the Run** and **Crazy Comets**. These are Newman's published musical and technical interpretations, not fresh listening results or statements of composer intent from this session. The important distinction is between perceived ensemble size and physical simultaneous oscillator count.

The editor lineage reinforces that distinction. The [1989 Rob Hubbard Editor release](https://csdb.dk/release/?id=75124) credits **Predator/Geir Tjelta of Moz(IC)art** as its coder; a contributor identifies the underlying routine as Hubbard's ACE II player. It is a scene tool around a driver, not evidence of Hubbard's own historical authoring interface. Laxity's first-person recollection reproduced in [SID Media Lab](https://akaobi.wordpress.com/2013/09/03/introducing-rob-hubbard/) describes manual pointer/table relocation, a later hexadecimal editor, and subsequent evolution toward pulse/filter programs and more reliable restart handling. His account is testimony about his learning and tools, rather than permission to attribute those later techniques to Hubbard's earliest player.

## Galway: a source-language instrument and a rapid audition system

Galway's original code is now available. His [composer-owned C64_music repository](https://github.com/MartinGalway/C64_music) was published with a statement dated **2026-04-14** and carries GPL-3.0. This session inspected revision `a458a3687e27a63647bbe41968094f0e6d0965a0`. It contains Rambo loading music, Arkanoid and its separate drum program, Green Beret, Wizball, and Ocean assembler directives. Galway identifies Wizball with his first-generation player and Athena as the beginning of his second generation. Older accounts relying entirely on disassemblies therefore need updating; the sources do not automatically establish that every released game version is byte-identical to these files.

Peter Clarke's [2020 interview](https://remix64.com/interviews/an-interview-with-peter-clarke.html) gives a participant's description of the **Ocean Development System**: assembly source lived on a C128, assembled code transferred to a linked C64, and a target crash did not destroy the source. He describes separate channel data containing presets, notes, and effects, followed by reassembly to audition a change. This was a sophisticated development-and-listening loop despite its austere textual interface. The [Ocean directive notes](https://github.com/MartinGalway/C64_music/blob/a458a3687e27a63647bbe41968094f0e6d0965a0/ocean_assembler_directives.txt) corroborate transfer-and-run support through `ENT`, byte/word definitions, expression operators, and shorthand arithmetic instructions. These notes also mark two directives as uncertain; those entries should not be invented into a complete modern assembler specification.

The original [Rambo loader source](https://github.com/MartinGalway/C64_music/blob/a458a3687e27a63647bbe41968094f0e6d0965a0/rambload.asm) makes the musical language concrete. Commands include calls, transposed calls, return, repeat/next, chord tables, frequency and pulse programs, and direct updates to data and sounding state. A code command can transfer execution to a 6502 routine. The source's opening explicitly invokes such routines; its chord tables contain pitch offsets including the octave, while its pulse processor carries state across stages of a program. Per-voice call/loop stacks and shared phrases compress recurring material without flattening every frame into copied data. This is source inspection, not a claim that the driver supplies unlimited stack depth, universally safe command execution, or all modern tracker effects.

In Sean Masterson's [Zzap!64 interview](https://www.zzap64.co.uk/zzap10/martin.html), Galway favors composing inside the machine's capabilities over transferring material that the voices cannot perform. He also discusses filter inconsistency between machines and the memory budget for Rambo's many themes. His later [2001 Remix64 interview](https://remix64.com/interviews/interview-martin-galway.html) describes **Short Circuit** as taking four weeks of data entry and emphasizes developing unfamiliar sounds, while acknowledging limited polyphony and insufficient revision time. The inference for our workflow is to make short-section audition cheap: a correct importer alone cannot decide when a sustained accompaniment should yield to an expressive lead, or whether a new timbre justifies changing the orchestration.

The original [arkadrum.asm](https://github.com/MartinGalway/C64_music/blob/a458a3687e27a63647bbe41968094f0e6d0965a0/arkadrum.asm) is decisive for the second distinction. It computes successive low-nibble volume values using arithmetic and delay loops, writing physical `$D418`; a RAM symbol also named `D418` holds mode information to restore. Its main loop services drum playback while a raster interrupt calls the ordinary three-voice music and sound routines. These are CPU-generated waveforms through the volume path, not evidence of a fourth SID oscillator. The [HVSC/STIL account reproduced by SLAY Radio](https://www.slayradio.org/sidinfo/MUSICIANS/G/Galway_Martin/Arkanoid.sid) quotes Galway distinguishing the shipped synthesized drums from actual sample data he obtained later. A title-screen technique can consume resources that an in-game player cannot spare.

## Model, resource and tooling consequences

Header inspection of the five downloaded archives found **PAL/6581 flags `$14`**, not the **PAL/8580 `$24`** used for this project's arrangement. Arkanoid is **RSID**, whereas the other four are PSID. Preserve those targets when studying the originals; changing a model declaration is not a sonic conversion. The [primary reSID filter implementation](https://github.com/libsidplayfp/resid/blob/master/filter.cc) records distinct measured 6581/8580 transfer functions and different resonance models. That supports model-specific evaluation, not a promise that an emulator matches every physical specimen. Ring/sync relations and the shared filter also mean a supposedly isolated instrument may depend on the other voice's state; see the [manufacturer register reference](../sources.md#s01).

**Adoption recommendation:** use [SID Factory II](https://github.com/Chordian/sidfactory2/blob/master/README.md) or another established SID-native tracker as the composition environment, then evaluate its exported runtime through a compatible native validation path. SID Factory II documents multiple Laxity/JCH drivers, model/region selection, editable demo tunes, packing/relocation, and pulse/filter visualizers. These are capabilities absent from a static three-instrument score exporter. Its available source/examples and editor runtime still need component-level license and compatibility inspection before integration. This is a recommendation to evaluate an existing musical instrument, not a claim that its files already pass this repository's exporter-specific manifest verifier.

The following are **proposed experiments**, not implemented features or assertions about the masters' intentions:

- **Hubbard transient study:** author an original noise-to-pulse percussion patch in the selected tracker. Compare attacks across registers, one-voice PCM, and the full mix. Require exact waveform order, deterministic gate behavior on repeated notes, and bounded pitch descent; judge whether the bass and drums still articulate separately.
- **Galway sustained-tone study:** author a two-stage pulse program with independently timed delayed vibrato. Test legato transitions and instrument restart separately. Reject clicks from unintended pulse discontinuities; preserve deliberate discontinuities when they are part of the sound.
- **Arrangement study:** compare fixed lead/bass/harmony assignment against deliberate percussion or echo substitutions in rests. Keep the melody recognizable and record every voice reassignment. The success criterion is a clear musical role in the mix, not maximum register activity.
- **Resource study:** measure worst-case play cycles when all three notes change, multiple effects advance, and a pattern boundary is crossed simultaneously. Compare code/data size and raster spikes, not only average cost. The present full arrangement's measured maximum was 1334 of 19656 PAL cycles on 2026-10-12; this is our player measurement, not a historical Hubbard/Galway performance figure.
- **Model study:** render the same original patch under both modeled chips, with a stated filter curve, output gain, and digi configuration; audition and record differences. Keep CPU/register correctness, modeled audio, and physical-chip listening as distinct evidence.

The next-song improvement should therefore be an editable instrument-and-arrangement workflow with rapid audition and preserved SID-native exports. The existing note importer remains useful for source fidelity and an initial reduction. It should not become the mandatory interchange format for music whose identity resides in waveform programs, pulse trajectories, filter routing, modulation dependencies, or audio-rate register writes.
