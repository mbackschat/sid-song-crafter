# Tim Follin and Ben Daglish: composition, instruments and authoring tools

Researched and sources accessed: 2026-10-12. This study combines published composer interviews, a driver programmer's development record and archive attribution. It proposes practical experiments for this repository; it does not claim that the original SID binaries were traced or that their audio was heard in this session. The historical facts and the proposed experiments are identified separately. See the [research source register](../sources.md), [hardware study](../sid/hardware.md) and [composition study](../composition/instruments-and-composition.md) for the wider context.

The strongest shared lesson is that a successful score and a successful SID instrument are developed together. Entering the right notes establishes a transcription. A convincing SID arrangement also needs a performance vocabulary, changing textures, intentional voice allocation and fast iteration through the target sound engine. Follin and Daglish provide different, complementary examples of that working relationship.

## Tim Follin: let the sound suggest the composition

### What the published accounts establish

In the 2011 interview by Marcus Vinicius Garrett Chiado and Richard Atkinson, preserved in Takashi Kawano's Japanese translation, Follin describes composing by entering numeric note and duration data into a compiler. He connected a Tatung Einstein development machine to a real C64 and could hear a new build almost immediately. He associated his melodic approach with monophonic whistles and ocarinas, whose relatively simple tone and ornaments suited a single C64 channel. Across the drivers discussed, he describes slides, vibrato and automatic note alternation; this does not establish every effect in a specific C64 tune. The same account describes cautious filter use: broad motion or extreme settings rather than a presumed portable cutoff frequency. He acknowledged that a very low filtered bass could disappear on another SID model. These are recollections of his workflow, not a complete specification of any particular released player. [Follin interview, 2011, translated by Kawano](https://note.com/aka_obi/n/nbcecdeb83077).

Authorship of the music and authorship of the driver must remain separate. In the contemporary 1990 interview, Follin identifies Stephen Ruddy as the C64 routine programmer rather than his brother Mike. Ruddy's own interview describes Follin specifying the musical behaviour and Ruddy implementing it. His development video is presented as different stages and versions of Software Creations' C64 driver. Together, these records support collaboration with evolving tools; they do not support assigning one immutable engine to every Follin soundtrack. The video is a useful primary artifact to examine, but no visual or auditory measurements from it are claimed here. [Follin interview, 1990, translated by Kawano](https://note.com/aka_obi/n/n687adbf03082), [Ruddy's interview](https://www.c64.com/interviews/ruddy.html), [Ruddy's driver development video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=M0VY1ACtscM).

A more recent interview reports an approach in which each line has its own musical interest and each voice's next note matters. It also distinguishes adapting recognisable arcade material from introducing his own directions. The interview's discussion of hexadecimal MS-DOS authoring and Ruddy's driver explicitly concerns the NES; it is not evidence for C64 memory addresses or SID synthesis. This distinction matters because accounts of Follin's work regularly cross several machines in one paragraph. [Follin interview with Yacine Synapsas](https://yacinesynapsas.com/interview-tim-follin/).

Kawano's published retrospective connects Follin's influence and reception to a broader chipmusic practice. It is useful secondary analysis, especially for framing a chip as an instrument and locating authoring tools within a musical community. It cannot establish the waveform, update rate or oscillator routing of a particular C64 sound. [Kawano, “Anti-Memoirs: An Experience of Chipmusic in Japan”](https://akaobi.wordpress.com/2015/07/01/anti-memoirs-an-experience-of-chipmusic-in-japan/).

### Proposed Follin-inspired experiments

**A singing lead with restrained modulation.** Begin with a triangle lead, a short but nonzero attack and an audible release. Compare the same eight-bar phrase in three versions: fixed pitch; vibrato that begins only after the initial attack; and selected slides into phrase-ending notes. As a starting experiment, use roughly eight cents of vibrato depth with a five-hertz cycle, then adjust by ear. These numbers are proposed listening variables, not transcribed Follin settings. Keep the musical timing identical so that the comparison isolates performance. A successful result should preserve pitch identity and phrase shape while adding motion; constant wide vibrato that obscures the melody fails that criterion.

**Ornament as musical syntax.** Add a short neighbour note only at selected cadences or repeated-note turns. Test the ornament in isolation and in the full arrangement. The listener should still identify the principal note and the beat. At PAL frame resolution, a one-frame ornament already occupies approximately 20 ms; demanding a shorter duration requires a different scheduler, rather than quietly dropping the ornament. Retain exact gate-write checks because a quick retrigger can disappear between sampled snapshots.

**Independent accompaniment.** Give the bass its own contour and rhythmic identity, and let the remaining voice alternate between a response, a held tone and silence. Compare this with three voices moving in parallel on every melody note. The acceptance question is whether the melody remains intelligible and whether the supporting lines can be followed when soloed. This is an arrangement experiment, not an instruction to fill every available slot. A clear entrance after a rest can make a small synthesizer sound larger than permanent maximum density.

**A model-aware bass comparison.** First establish an unfiltered bass whose register pitch and PCM output are verified. Then audition a broad low-pass sweep and a very low static cutoff on both 6581 and 8580 emulation. Record the model and retain the original level in the comparison. Reject a patch whose musical bass disappears on the declared target. The interview explains why this experiment matters; it does not provide a model-independent magic cutoff.

## Ben Daglish: a memorable instrument can organise a whole piece

### What the published accounts establish

Daglish's 2018 interview describes an early collaboration with Tony Crowther in which written staff notation became letter names and numeric durations that Crowther could enter. Daglish later entered music directly himself. He identifies melody and a lively rhythmic feel as central interests, and says that discovering a satisfying SID cowbell led him to build *Deflektor* around that sound. This is unusually direct evidence for timbre generating composition. The interview also warns against trusting retrospective game lists without checking archives: he remembered tunes more readily than product names. [Daglish, “Music has always been in me”, Nerdy Nocą](https://nerdynoca.pl/blog/ben-daglish-interview-music-has-always-been-in-me/).

In the 1998 interview by Richard Karsmakers, Daglish describes players developed in consultation with programmers: he requested musical capabilities and they implemented them. He reports a cross-development workflow and says that C64 sounds could become the reference for an Amiga approximation. He also describes the painstaking transcription required for a fast guitar solo. This supports treating instrument control and musical accuracy as distinct tasks with distinct tools. It does not identify a unique Daglish editor that covered every game. [Karsmakers' Daglish interview](https://www.karsmakers.nl/metal-e-zine/benn.htm).

The Lemon64 interview adds several boundaries: text entry was central; fitting drums, bass and strings into three voices was a real arrangement challenge; he avoided precise static filtering in favour of sweeps because machines differed; and he says he did not use digitised samples on the C64. That last statement prevents a metallic or convincing percussive sound from being casually labelled a sample. A sound's apparent realism cannot establish its implementation. [Lemon64 interview with Daglish](https://www.lemon64.com/page/ben-daglish-interview).

In the 2001 Remix64 interview, Daglish identifies *Trap* as an early extended composition with introductory and concluding material and an orchestral ambition. He also describes collaborating with Rob Hubbard on *Auf Wiedersehen Monty* through several days of musical work together. His appreciation of SID's analogue character coexists with frustration at its limited polyphony and inconsistent filtering. The appropriate lesson is to design form and orchestration within the constraints, not to treat all his work as one preset collection. [Neil Carr's Daglish interview, 2001](https://remix64.com/interviews/interview-ben-daglish.html).

### Proposed Daglish-inspired experiments

**Build a percussion identity before adding a full rhythm track.** Design a short metallic hit and write an eight-bar motif that gives it a recognisable placement. Compare a triangle-based metallic experiment, a brief pulse-based attack and a noise attack with a pitched body. These are candidate instruments; none is asserted to reproduce Daglish's historical cowbell. If an experiment needs ring modulation or sync, record the oscillator dependency and the musical voice sacrificed. Count that cost before deciding that the patch fits a three-voice arrangement.

**Make rhythm survive the reduction.** Write the essential bass and percussion placements first, then add a lead with deliberate off-beat answers and short rests. Compare three versions: percussion everywhere, percussion only at structural accents, and no percussion. Judge clarity of pulse, melodic continuity and contrast at the same playback level. The most elaborate version should win only if it strengthens the musical result. An instrument that is impressive alone but repeatedly cuts the bass at important harmonic arrivals is an arrangement failure.

**Use section-dependent instruments.** Keep one recognisable melodic motive while changing register, articulation or supporting texture between an opening, development and return. Test the final transition and cadence as carefully as the first ten seconds. The listener should recognise both recurrence and change. Instrument variation should serve this distinction; random waveform changes do not constitute development.

## What the tooling decision should preserve

These histories support adopting a mature SID composition tool for instrumental work. They also support retaining this repository's reproducible export and verification path. A bespoke note player is valuable as a small, inspectable execution target; developing a competitive instrument editor, modulation tables, phrase tools and an audition workflow is a different project.

For a candidate editor, the practical evaluation is whether an eight-bar test can express a delayed vibrato, a slide, a percussion attack and a planned texture change, then export a callable player that remains testable. The workflow should make it easy to solo voices, replay a selected phrase, retain an instrument revision and compare models. Those are musical iteration requirements, not proof that either historical composer used the same modern application. Select the tool on the demonstrated experiment and the target contract, rather than on whether its interface resembles an imagined 1980s workstation.

The current baseline player's fixed waveform and ADSR per voice can validate score reduction and timing. It does not yet encode the trajectories proposed above. The next sound-design decision should therefore be made with short, level-matched audio comparisons and a declared target SID model. Preserve the complete score, exact writes and ending guards while evaluating richer authoring tools. Sound quality needs an explicit listening judgment alongside the automated execution evidence.
